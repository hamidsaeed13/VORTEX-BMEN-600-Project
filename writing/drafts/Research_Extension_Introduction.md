# Draft Introduction synthesis — for team integration

Opportunistic screening may use information already collected during routine care to identify
people who warrant glycemic testing. CBC is a frequently ordered laboratory panel, but its
availability does not imply that it can safely substitute for a glycemic test. The relevant
question is whether CBC adds useful information beyond patient characteristics already
available at the same encounter. Cost savings also depend on the actual ordering workflow
and the consequences of missed cases, neither of which is measured by NHANES.

The closest CBC-based prediction study by Guo and colleagues combines hematological indices
with age, sex, BMI and derived inflammatory ratios, and evaluates transport to a later calendar
year. Its HbA1c threshold includes prediabetes and its population includes established cases;
it therefore does not directly answer the question of diabetes-range HbA1c among adults without
a reported diagnosis. Its use of BMI is especially relevant: a comparison against demographics
without anthropometry could overstate the incremental contribution of CBC.
[Guo et al.](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59213-3)

Liu and colleagues have already studied undiagnosed-diabetes identification using NHANES
and non-CBC anthropometric, demographic and behavioral information. Their combined glycemic
endpoint differs from an HbA1c-only endpoint, and their model is not a direct benchmark for
the present cohort. Together, these studies motivate a focused incremental comparison rather
than a claim that machine-learning diabetes screening itself is new.
[Liu et al.](https://doi.org/10.2196/68260)

Biological interpretation is complicated because red-cell properties can affect HbA1c
independently of glucose. Evidence summarized by English and colleagues motivates sensitivity
analyses involving low hemoglobin and a separate glucose outcome. Such analyses cannot remove
all effects of iron deficiency, altered red-cell survival or hemoglobin variants, but they can
test whether an apparent HbA1c association is robust to important alternative explanations.
[English et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-015-3599-3)

We therefore examine whether CBC adds discrimination beyond demographics and BMI for an
observed HbA1c of at least 6.5% among NHANES adults without a reported diabetes diagnosis.
We compare matched predictor sets with survey-weighted fitting and nested cluster-grouped
validation, assess biological and endpoint sensitivity, and evaluate frozen models in an
independent earlier NHANES cycle. The contribution is an assessment of incremental value,
uncertainty and robustness, including potentially negative findings. This cross-sectional
analysis does not establish confirmed new diabetes, prospective clinical benefit, economic
savings or a safe policy for withholding HbA1c tests.
